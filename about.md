# About

I'm an Iranian-Canadian researcher currently calling the Bay Area home. The common thread running through my interests has always been language. My idea of fun as a teenager was reading Chomsky's *Syntactic Structures* and discovering that words like *daughter* and *دختر* share a common root (goosebumps!). I think about the inferior frontal gyrus, a lot.

I studied Linguistics and Film Studies at the University of British Columbia, with a minor in Spanish. I moved to Toronto in 2019 to start a Master's degree in Speech-Language Pathology at the University of Toronto. During my clinical placements, I was struck by how readily we look to the brain when language breaks down in adults, yet how rarely we do so when children struggle with learning language. That led me to reach out to Professor Kate Watkins at the University of Oxford, one of the few researchers at the time to have secured funding for a large-scale, multimodal investigation of developmental language disorder (DLD), integrating behavioural and brain imaging methods. I spent the next three years at Oxford as her PhD student, co-supervised by Profs Kate Nation (Oxford) and Saloni Krishnan (University College London), learning neuroimaging, neuroanatomy, and how to turn broad ideas into testable hypotheses.

My PhD focused on the behavioural and neural correlates of DLD, including a longitudinal study of myelin development in a subset of these children. My first paper from the thesis, published in *Neurobiology of Language*, showed that children with DLD had reduced cortical surface area in language- and reading-related cortical regions, and that smaller surface area was associated with poorer language ability. I later used functional MRI to investigate the role of frontostriatal circuitries in novel word-form learning in DLD, and, in another project, how dopaminergic reward systems are associated with learning words from written context in children with dyslexia.

In 2025, I moved to San Francisco to join Dr. Maria Luisa Gorno-Tempini's lab and the UCSF Dyslexia Center as a postdoctoral researcher, through the UCSF–UC Berkeley Schwab Program. I continue to study DLD and dyslexia using multimodal neuroimaging and in-depth cognitive phenotyping, with a broadened interest in how attention and executive functions interact with oral and written language systems. My recent work has examined the cognitive and white-matter mechanisms underlying the co-occurrence of dyslexia and ADHD. I also contributed to data-driven approaches for identifying cognitive subtypes of dyslexia. I am currently designing a new fMRI paradigm to assess the neural correlates of phonological vs. morphological awareness in struggling learners.

I also have a soft spot for speech motor control. During the pandemic, I co-authored a review paper with Dr. Aravind Namasivayam and Prof. Van Lieshout on telehealth treatment for childhood apraxia of speech to help clinicians navigate the sudden shift to remote therapy. More recently, I contributed to a paper arguing that many of the categories we use to classify childhood speech sound disorders may reflect the limits of human perception rather than the continuous nature of speech motor development. I believe this perspective will shape the future of how we treat speech disorders in children.

When not working, I dance Argentine tango. It's not so different from language development. You begin by learning the building blocks, the individual steps, much like babbling speech sounds. Those combine into familiar movement patterns, just as sounds combine into words, until eventually you're able to string them together into longer, fluid sequences. With enough practice (and far longer than it takes children to learn language), technique fades into the background, opening room for subtlety and a chance to converse with another person on the dance floor.

Naturally, my tango obsession comes with the culture attached; I bake alfajores when I'm craving something sweet and spend most evenings sipping on yerba mate.
